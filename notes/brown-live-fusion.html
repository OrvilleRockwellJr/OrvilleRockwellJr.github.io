Two Raspberry Pi 5s, each on its own network link (Ethernet or Wi-Fi), stream synchronized capture into one page; each camera's status line shows frame rate, frame count and the synchronization offset between the two. The browser shows each stream with its own network delay, so on-screen frames are not frame-locked; timing comes from the capture logs.

The problem

One view is never enough

Localizing a fast-moving drone without satellite navigation is hard because motion blur and low texture starve any single view of features. The approach integrates many synchronized views with inertial cues, so every downstream estimate inherits the geometry and timing of the camera rig. Fusion is only as good as its timestamps: residual timing offsets between cameras and errors in the calibrated geometry between them turn directly into pose error, while the baselines and overlap between views set how much parallax each pair provides.

How the work is split

Three teams, one system

  • MechanicalDesigns and 3D-prints the frame that holds the cameras at fixed positions, since the geometry between cameras has to stay put for calibration to hold.
  • ElectricalBuilds the capture hardware: camera modules, synchronization boards, Raspberry Pi 5 compute and the wired links that bring the streams together.
  • SoftwareCalibrates the cameras, detects and tracks features, and estimates pose from the fused views and inertial cues.

Where the teams overlap

  • Vision meets electrical. Computer vision depends on how cameras are triggered, how frames are timestamped and how fast streams reach the processor, and the electrical design is judged by what the software can recover from it.
  • Geometry meets software. The mechanical frame sets the geometry the calibration software has to estimate.
  • Timing and geometry are the shared problem.

Design analysis

Camera layout and coverage

The rig is 16 cameras on a spherical frame in rings of 1-4-6-4-1, chosen because it was easier to design and to analyze for blind spots. Every camera uses an 80° × 65° field of view.

Wider lens winsAgainst 70°×55° cameras, the 80°×65° lens saw far more of a 12-inch checkerboard 2 m from the smallest three-camera triangle; only the middle squares were blind, close to the theoretical prediction.
Smaller propeller footprintAt 0.5, 1 and 2 ft the propellers took up a smaller share of each 80°×65° view than of a 70°×55° view.
Propeller placementBetween the middle and second rings obstructs least, because the cameras there face away from the propellers.

To compare designs, camera positions and angles from the CAD frame were projected onto 650,000 test points spread over the upper hemisphere 2 m from the frame, so each point receives the pixel that sees it. Coverage is the share of points seen by at least one camera; stereo is the share seen by two or more.

What I did

My part

My work started with the geometry of how propellers block the cameras, and moved to the multi-camera sensing layer the visual pipeline rests on, which sits at the overlap above.

  1. Propeller obstruction versus camera standoff. A field-of-view model of how much of a propeller a three-camera triangle sees, used to size the standoff and to argue for smaller propellers.
  2. Camera calibration and hardware bring-up of a synchronized camera array on a Raspberry Pi 5.
  3. Multi-camera fusion software. I wrote the software that fuses two Raspberry Pi 5s on separate network links into one capture, shown in the recording above, with frame-level time synchronization.
  4. Now: scaling the rig to 16 cameras across the two Pis, with latency tests, and contributing calibration and test code to the group's repository.

What I built

The fusion software

The group's camera-sync code drove up to 8 cameras from a single Raspberry Pi. A 16-camera rig needs two Pis, and that moves the problem from one board to two machines on separate network links, each with its own clock and its own stream. The software below is what I wrote to make those two Pis behave as one camera system for this rig.

  • What it doesTakes the synchronized capture from each Pi over its own network link (Ethernet, or Wi-Fi in some tests) and fuses the two into one capture with frame-level time synchronization, so a frame from one Pi can be compared with the matching frame from the other.
  • What the live view reportsFor every camera: frame rate (about 30.5 fps in the recording), frame count, the synchronization offset between the cameras in microseconds, camera timeouts and restarts. Those are the numbers the downstream pose estimation depends on.
  • Why it matters for the researchFusion is only as good as its timestamps. Cross-Pi synchronization is the step that lets the rig grow beyond one board, which is what a 16-camera layout requires. This is the first stage: the sensing layer that visual localization will sit on.
  • NextAdd the second sync board on each Pi to reach 16 cameras, then measure latency by moving a target across the shared view from different positions and averaging across cameras.

Analysis

Propeller size versus camera standoff

Three cameras on a wall plane, each 80° × 65° (half-angles 40° and 32.5°), so at depth z a camera sees a window 2z·tan 40° wide and 2z·tan 32.5° tall. The propeller is a disc of diameter D centered on the triangle centroid. Assumes an equilateral triangle, parallel optical axes and a disc parallel to the wall.

  • Visible propeller areaAbout 0.367·D²/z² of the view. At 18 in that is 11.3% for a 10 in propeller and 4.1% for 6 in; at 24 in it is 6.4% and 2.3%.
  • All three cameras see the whole propellerOnce z ≥ (0.866s + D)/1.274, with s the triangle side. For a 10 in propeller the largest triangle needs about 24 in of standoff (s ≈ 24 in) and the smallest about 10 in (s ≈ 3 in).
  • Why shrink the propellersEach inch of diameter costs 0.785 in of standoff. 10 in to 8 in saves 1.6 in; 10 in to 6 in saves 3.1 in and cuts visible area by 64%.
