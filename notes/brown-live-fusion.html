GPS-Denied Visual Navigation for Drones

Prof. Benjamin Kimia's group, LEMS, Brown University

Screen recording, 25 seconds, no sound. The live fusion view of the multi-camera rig. Two Raspberry Pi 5s, each on its own Ethernet link, stream synchronized capture into one page; the status line for each camera shows its frame rate, frame count and the synchronization offset between the two. The browser shows each stream with its own network delay, so on-screen frames are not frame-locked; timing comes from the capture logs.

The problem

Localizing a fast-moving drone without satellite navigation is hard because motion blur and low texture starve any single view of features. The approach integrates many synchronized views with inertial cues, so every downstream estimate inherits the geometry and timing of the camera rig. Fusion is only as good as its timestamps: residual timing offsets between cameras and errors in the calibrated geometry between them turn directly into pose error, while the baselines and overlap between views set how much parallax each pair provides.

Three teams, one system

  • MechanicalDesigns and 3D-prints the frame that holds the cameras at fixed positions, since the geometry between cameras has to stay put for calibration to hold.
  • ElectricalBuilds the capture hardware: camera modules, synchronization boards, Raspberry Pi 5 compute and the wired links that bring the streams together.
  • SoftwareCalibrates the cameras, detects and tracks features, and estimates pose from the fused views and inertial cues.

The teams overlap most where vision meets hardware. Computer vision depends on electrical choices (how cameras are triggered, how frames are timestamped, how fast streams reach the processor), and the electrical design is judged by what the software can recover from it. The mechanical frame sets the geometry the calibration software has to estimate. Timing and geometry are the shared problem.

Camera layout and coverage

The rig is designed as 16 cameras on a spherical frame in rings of 1-4-6-4-1, chosen because it was easier to design and to analyze for blind spots. Every camera uses an 80° × 65° field of view. In side-by-side tests against 70° × 55° cameras, the wider lens saw far more of a 12-inch checkerboard placed 2 m from the smallest three-camera triangle (only the middle squares were blind, close to the theoretical prediction), and propellers took up a smaller share of each view at 0.5, 1 and 2 ft. Placing the propellers between the middle and second rings obstructs the least, because the cameras there face away from them.

To compare designs, the camera positions and angles from the CAD frame were projected onto 650,000 test points spread over the upper hemisphere 2 m from the frame, so each point receives the pixel that sees it. Coverage is the share of points seen by at least one camera; stereo is the share seen by two or more.

My part

I own the multi-camera sensing layer the visual pipeline rests on, which sits at the overlap above: hardware bring-up of a synchronized camera array on a Raspberry Pi 5, camera calibration, and frame-level time synchronization. I wrote the software that fuses two Raspberry Pi 5s on separate Ethernet links into one capture, shown in the recording above, and I am scaling the rig to 16 cameras across the two Pis, with latency tests. I have contributed calibration and test code to the group's repository.
