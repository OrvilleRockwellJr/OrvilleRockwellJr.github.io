Better-calibrated scores. Worse discoveries.

A self-driving lab is a control loop: a model proposes candidates, a system tests a batch, and the results retrain the model. I simulated that loop over five ChEMBL targets and asked whether calibrating the scoring model makes it more efficient. For greedy selection it does not, and on two of five targets it hurts.

The question

Does calibration make the loop more efficient?

The common intuition is that better-calibrated probabilities should help, since the selection step consumes them as if they were true. I tested it directly: the same loop with and without isotonic calibration of the scoring model, counting actives recovered within a fixed budget. Five ChEMBL targets spanning a wide range of active prevalence (PTP1B, AChE, HDAC1, EGFR, JAK2), 20 random seeds each, 400 experiments per run.

What happened

Isotonic never helped greedy selection

Each bar is the change in hits recovered compared with ranking by the raw score. Amber is isotonic calibration; teal is isotonic with a raw-score tie-break. A star marks p < 0.01 in a paired test over 20 seeds.

The loss tracks the size of the tied plateau at the top of the ranking, from 88 molecules on PTP1B to 2,212 on JAK2 (correlation −0.70). Isotonic also lowered ROC-AUC on every target, by 0.030 on average, even as calibration error fell. Platt and beta calibration, which are strictly monotone, made exactly the raw selections.

Why

Calibration can trade away resolution

Isotonic regression fits the monotone function that best maps scores to observed outcomes:

f* = argmin over non-decreasing f of ∑i (yi − f(si))²

On finite data the solution is a step function: adjacent candidates whose labels disagree with their order are pooled into a single level. That never reverses two candidates, but it can merge them, and merged candidates cannot be ranked against each other. The selection step reads only the top of the ranking. When hundreds or thousands of molecules share the maximum score and the batch is smaller than that, the model’s remaining ordering information is discarded at the one place it mattered.

Schematic. Raw scores rise smoothly with rank; the isotonic fit is a staircase, and the batch is drawn from the top step.

What recovers it

Three fixes, one lesson

Break ties with the raw scoreRank by calibrated probability first and raw score second. It returned greedy selection to raw levels on every target (JAK2: 383.9 versus 384.3), a direct causal test.
Use a strictly monotone calibratorPlatt and beta calibration preserve the full ranking while still correcting overconfidence.
Evaluate the decisionReport hits at budget and enrichment at the top of the ranking alongside calibration error. The two can move in opposite directions.

The failure is general: a pipeline that calibrates and then ranks can lose discrimination at the decision boundary while every calibration metric improves. The metric that matters is the one the decision consumes.

Status and limits

What this is, and is not

This is a manuscript in preparation reporting a preliminary result, not a submitted or published paper.

  • A retrospective simulation on curated ChEMBL actives (pChEMBL ≥ 6), not a wet-lab campaign; assay noise is abstracted away.
  • One scorer family (logistic regression on ECFP4 fingerprints), so the calibrator is the only variable. A non-linear scorer may behave differently, though the step-function argument does not depend on the model.
  • Diversity-aware acquisition and a bound on plateau size versus hit loss are future work, and ECE is a coarse metric that should be checked with proper scoring rules.
