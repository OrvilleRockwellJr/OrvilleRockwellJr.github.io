Method

The target

After a stroke, the glial scar around the injury is rich in chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs). Neurons carry a receptor that reads them, the phosphatase PTPσ (gene PTPRS), and binding it stalls the growth cone (Shen et al., 2009, Science 326:592–596). Blocking PTPσ with a mimetic peptide improved recovery in spinal cord injury (Lang et al., 2015, Nature 518:404–408) and in a mouse intracerebral-hemorrhage stroke model (Yao et al., 2022, J Neuroinflammation 19:207). Two routes to the same protein are testable in silico: a small molecule that inhibits it and reaches the brain, or a guide RNA that removes it. This platform designs candidates for both.

One engine, two alphabets

Both modalities use the same code (engine.js, mirrored from gd.py). A denoising network predicts the noise in a corrupted code xt (DDPM training, Ho et al., 2020; cosine schedule, 200 steps; DDIM sampling, Song et al., 2021). Steering follows classifier guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021):

ε̂ = εθ(xt, t) − √(1−ᾱt) · γ · Σk wk gk ∇x log pk(yk | xt)

  • Molecules. A 1.2M-parameter GRU autoencoder maps SMILES to a 64-dimensional latent code (Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018, ACS Cent Sci 4:268–276). Diffusion runs in that latent space, as in latent diffusion for images (Rombach et al., 2022, CVPR). A 1.9M-parameter denoiser was trained on the codes of 148,528 drug-like molecules (ZINC subset plus the labeled sets, no labels used). The decoder turns the final code back into a SMILES string; RDKit checks validity.
  • Gene-editing guides. The code is the one-hot of a 30-nt SpCas9 site (4 nt, 20-nt protospacer, NGG, 3 nt) scaled to ±1 and read out by argmax at the end. A 1.2M-parameter denoiser was trained on 111,796 NGG-bearing sites from the coding sequence of 419 random human genes (Ensembl, GRCh38). The held-out genes below were excluded.

Critics trained on noise

A classifier that has only seen clean inputs gives meaningless gradients on a half-noised code. Each critic is therefore trained on xt at random noise levels. For each property I train an ensemble of five small networks on bootstrap resamples. Guidance uses the ensemble's mean logit, never a rank or an isotonic-calibrated score: in my closed-loop study, isotonic calibration collapsed the top of the ranking into ties and cost hits, while raw scores kept the resolution a gradient needs.

  • Brain penetration: critic on B3DB compounds that are not in BBBP, 1,465 molecules after the length filter (Meng et al., 2021, Sci Data 8). The judge uses BBBP (Martins et al., 2012, J Chem Inf Model 52:1686–1697), 1,357 molecules, so the two share no compounds.
  • PTP-family activity: ChEMBL IC50/Ki/Kd for PTPN1, PTPN11, PTPRF, PTPRC, PTPN2, PTPN6, PTPRB, PTPN22, PTPRG and PTPRS (Mendez et al., 2019, NAR 47:D930–D940); active if pChEMBL ≥ 5.5, inactive if < 5.0; 1,664 molecules. PTPσ itself has only 30 distinct molecules with exact potencies, 15 of which fit this model's length limit. Critic and judge both come from ChEMBL, so this objective is not independently audited.
  • Toxicity: critic on Tox21 (flagged if active in any of 12 assays), 5,906 molecules after filtering. The judge uses ToxCast (Richard et al., 2016, Chem Res Toxicol 29:1225–1251), 2,903 molecules, label = share of non-Tox21 assays in which it is active above the 75th percentile (11%). Different assays; some compounds appear in both sets.
  • Guide efficiency, critic: the Kim et al. 2019 SpCas9 library (DeepSpCas9; Sci Adv 5:eaax9249) as compiled with CRISPRon's data (Xiang et al., 2021, Nat Commun 12:3238), 12,784 guides plus 526 test guides in HEK293T (11,458 train, 1,326 validation; the 526 test guides are held out); label = above the median indel frequency.
  • Guide efficiency, judge: the 10,543 guides newly measured for CRISPRon (Xiang et al., 2021; labelled “Luo 2020” in the released file), a different library and lab (8,464 train); no guide appears in both sets. Both models are also scored on the Doench et al. activity screens (2014, Nat Biotechnol 32:1262–1267; 2016, Nat Biotechnol 34:184–191), a third assay, using the 1,080 guides from five genes the project already held out.

The trust gate

The question behind the project is when a steered generator should trust its critic. The gate answers it at every step: if the five heads of an ensemble disagree (standard deviation s of their logits), that objective's push is scaled by 1/(1 + (s/τ)²). Where the heads agree, guidance is full strength; where they split, usually off the data the critic was trained on, it fades. The Evidence tab compares gate on and off with the same noise.

Aiming guides at a locus

To make guides specific to a gene, a locus critic is added to the efficiency critic. Its library is every NGG site whose cut falls inside a PTPRS exon (or inside DNA you paste). It is not a trained classifier but the exact diffusion score of a mixture of those sites: at noise level t, log p(xt) = logsumexpj(wj − ||xt − √ᾱt sj||² / 2v). With "favor efficient sites" on, the weights wj are the efficiency critic's log-probabilities, so the pull lands on good sites. It has no ensemble spread, so the trust gate does not apply to it. Because it is retrieval, a known-locus scan is a stronger baseline; the Evidence tab reports both.

The request box

The text box on the first tab is read by a small set of rules that run in your browser, not by a language model and not by any API. It looks for words about modality (molecule, guide, CRISPR), objectives (brain, potency, toxicity, including "ignore"), strength (gentle, hard), a count, a gene name and any pasted DNA, shows you exactly what it matched, and lets you change every setting before anything runs. Words it does not recognize only label the report.

Per-base reports

Every guide can open a report. Mutagenesis: each of the 90 single-base changes is scored by the critic ensemble and by the held-out judge, and the cell shows the change in predicted efficiency. Prior surprise: the guide is noised at four levels and the coding-sequence prior tries to denoise it; the error at each position is its surprise. This is our analogue of the per-base entropy Evo Designer shows, but from a small denoiser, so read it as “looks like the training sequences”, not as evolutionary constraint. The percentile compares the guide with 120 random PTPRS cut sites. Disagreement: among positions where either model sees a gain of 0.03 or more, how many have a different best base for the critic and the judge.

Where this sits

This is not a genome model. Evo 2 is steered by generating many sequences and keeping those an outside predictor favors. Here the predictor's gradient acts at every denoising step, with an uncertainty gate, and a second model that never saw the steering audits the result. The question it is built to test is when a steering predictor can be trusted, and when guidance beats generate-and-filter. Results so far are mixed and are in the Evidence tab: the gate matters for molecules and not for guides.

The held-out judge

Guidance can only look good by the critic's own standard if the generator learns the critic's blind spots. So every result is also scored by a judge that shares no features or model class with the critic: logistic regression on ECFP4 fingerprints for molecules, logistic regression on positional one-hot and dinucleotide features for guides. Version 2 of this project also trains the judge on different data from the critic. For guides, the critic learned from one lab's library and the judge from another's. For brain penetration and toxicity, the judge's compounds or assays differ from the critic's. The activity objective is the exception: both models come from ChEMBL. Independent data does not make the judge correct, only less likely to repeat the critic's mistakes; it is still a small model. The judge never sees the generator or the critic. The gap between the two is the reward-hacking signal.

What changed from version 1

Version 1 trained critic and judge on the same labels, with the 4,379 Doench guides (2,888 for training) and one B3DB split for both molecule models. Version 2 uses 23,902 guides from two labs and separate compound sets for brain penetration and toxicity. I also tried a longer-trained molecule denoiser (40,000 steps) because only 36% of unguided samples decode to valid structures. Unguided validity rose to 40%, but guided sampling became unstable (validity fell to 3–12% without the gate), so I reverted it. The headline numbers on the site come from the version-1 denoiser with version-2 critics and judges.

Splits

Molecules: Bemis–Murcko scaffold split, 80/10/10, so test chemistry is new. Guides: the critic's test set is the 526 Kim 2019 test guides; the judge's is a random 10% of the Luo 2020 library (1,051 guides, shared guides excluded); the Doench genes CD13, CD45, NF2, THY1 and CD28 (never used for training either model) give a third, cross-assay test. Random splits within a library are optimistic because neighbouring guides can come from the same gene, so the cross-lab numbers are the ones to trust.

Checks that the browser equals the research code

web/parity.js compares the JavaScript engine against the PyTorch reference on fixed inputs: denoiser outputs, critic means, spreads and input gradients, full guided sampling trajectories (gate on and off), and the SMILES decoder. The scripts and the saved results are in the repository.

Limits

  • These are small models on small public datasets. A high judge score means “looks like known actives to a fingerprint model,” not “works.”
  • The activity model learns PTP-family inhibition. Most known PTP inhibitors are polar phosphotyrosine mimetics that do not cross the blood–brain barrier, so the three objectives pull against each other; expect trade-offs rather than clean wins.
  • Generated guides are designed against efficiency features, not against the PTPRS locus. Use the scanner to score real PTPRS sites. Off-target effects are not modeled.
  • The efficiency labels come from specific cell types and Cas9 delivery conditions.
  • No synthesis, wet-lab or animal data are involved. Nothing here is medical advice.

Scope

This version covers two alphabets, small molecules and 30-nt guide sites, with one guidance engine. It does not yet cover proteins, peptides or genome-scale sequence; the guide generator models 30-nt windows, not whole loci. Adding longer-range sequence models is future work.

Reproduce

All code is in projects/diffusion-design/: data preparation, training, evaluation (scripts/), the browser engine (engine.js, worker.js) and the saved results (results/). Chemistry in the browser uses RDKit.js (BSD-3-Clause).

Photo reading and analog design (added later)

Photo. An uploaded image is read entirely in the browser: pixel statistics give a coarse image kind, a locally hosted Tesseract reads any text, and regular expressions pull out SMILES-like strings, DNA runs and gene names. Strings are accepted only if RDKit parses them (common OCR slips such as l, I and 0 for 1 and O are tried). There is no vision-language model: drawn structures, gels and scenes are not interpreted, and the diffusion model is not conditioned on pixels.

Analogs. The model's SMILES encoder now ships in the browser (parity with PyTorch to 1e-4). A seed molecule is encoded, noised to a chosen fraction of the schedule, then denoised with the same critic guidance and decoded (an SDEdit-style edit). A round-trip check reports whether the model can reproduce the seed at all; on four test molecules two round-tripped exactly and two did not, so analogs of molecules outside the training distribution drift.

Two-key pairs. The pair score is the geometric mean of a molecule score and a guide score. It is a ranking rule, not a validated predictor.

Decoder constraints and repair (added later)

PTPσ reference check. Because the learned critic is near chance on PTPσ, each molecule card also shows its closest active and closest inactive among the 60 PTPσ-tested ChEMBL molecules (Morgan radius 2, 1024 bits, Tanimoto). The rule "closest active minus closest inactive" was scored leaving out whole chemical series (12 Butina clusters at distance 0.6): AUC 0.73 (95% CI 0.59-0.86, n = 60, 23 active). That is better than the critic (0.61) but is a similarity rule on a tiny, series-heavy set, not a validated predictor, and generated molecules are mostly far from known PTPσ chemistry, so most read "no clear lean". Script: scripts/ptprs_ref.py.

The SMILES decoder is still the same trained GRU, but decoding is now constrained at inference: tokens that would unbalance a parenthesis, leave a ring-closure digit dangling, exceed an atom's valence or start a long aromatic or carbon run are masked, and each latent is decoded up to nine times (the original plus eight copies perturbed by 0.06 in standardized latent units); the first copy RDKit accepts is kept. On 200 guided generations this raised valid molecules from 47% to 66% and molecules passing the studio's sanity filter from 34 to 82. Validity is still not guaranteed: aromaticity and some valence cases slip through, a repaired molecule is a neighbor of the latent the critics scored (tagged "repaired decode"), and this is not a substitute for a decoder that is valid by construction. Decoder fine-tune. Diffusion samples sit slightly off the data manifold, where the decoder had never been trained. I froze the encoder (so the latent space, denoiser, critics and judges are unchanged) and fine-tuned only the decoder on latents with added noise (0 to 1 standardized unit) against the original SMILES, 7 minutes on CPU. Plain greedy decoding of noised held-out latents went from 55% to 89% valid at noise 0.3 and from 38% to 85% at 0.6 (n = 600, RDKit); clean reconstruction stayed at 91%. In the engine on 150 guided generations, the plain decode alone is now 76% valid (114/150) and with the constrained and perturbed retries 89% (133/150), up from 66%. Still not valid by construction.
