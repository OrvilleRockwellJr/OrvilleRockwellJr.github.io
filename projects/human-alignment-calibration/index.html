Preregistered study

Looks where humans look. Does it know when it’s wrong?

Networks that rely on the image regions people mark as diagnostic are not obviously better at knowing when they are wrong. I tested it on 1,500 ClickMe images and eight pretrained ImageNet networks, with the hypotheses and tests written down before any model was run.

The question

Is human-like evidence trustworthy evidence?

Fel et al. (2022) found across 85 networks that higher ImageNet accuracy came with lower alignment between a network’s feature-importance map and the map humans produce (ClickMe). Alignment is usually studied as a property of a model. Here it is a property of an image: for each image, how closely does the network’s evidence match the human’s, and does that predict whether its confidence can be trusted?

The null hypothesis is that correctness is independent of alignment once you know the confidence. If it holds, alignment adds nothing a confidence score does not already say.

Method

Specified before running

  1. DataA fixed random subset of 1,500 images from the ClickMe validation split, the same images for every model.
  2. ModelsAlexNet, VGG-16, ResNet-18, ResNet-50, DenseNet-121, EfficientNet-B0, ConvNeXt-Tiny, ViT-B/16 (torchvision pretrained weights).
  3. Evidence and alignmentAbsolute gradient of the true-class logit against the human map, Spearman correlation normalised by the human ceiling. Excess alignment subtracts the correlation with other images’ maps, removing the centre bias.
  4. TestWithin-bin AUROC of excess alignment for correctness across ten confidence bins, 5,000 permutations, Holm correction over eight models, plus the change in cross-validated AUROC when alignment is added to confidence.

Results

The trade-off holds. The reliability signal does not.

Accurate networks align less with humans. ViT-B/16 is the most accurate (93.1%) and sits at 0.04 of the human ceiling; AlexNet is the least accurate (69.2%) and the most aligned.

● confirmatory H2   ○ secondary H3. All H2 values lie between 0.467 and 0.520, Holm-adjusted p = 1.00 in every model.

H2Excess alignment does not predict correctness beyond confidence in any of the eight models. Cross-validated AUROC gains range from −0.0014 to +0.0010, and the only intervals that exclude zero are slightly negative.

H3The secondary anchoring index, how much the true-class probability falls when the human-marked pixels are masked, showed a weak signal in the newest architectures (ConvNeXt-Tiny, EfficientNet-B0, ViT-B/16; Holm p = 0.015, 0.028, 0.005). I then found a floor effect: for wrong predictions the true-class probability is already low, so the index cannot be large. The post-hoc variant that measures the predicted class leaves only ConvNeXt-Tiny below 0.05 (0.043). I report both, and mark the second as post hoc.

Reading it

What this does and does not say

Looking where humans look, at the level of single images, does not make a network’s confidence more trustworthy than the confidence already is. A plausible reading is that confidence already summarises how much evidence the network found, so a map-level similarity adds nothing about that. It does not say alignment is unimportant; it says alignment is not a free reliability signal for pretrained models.

  • 1,500 images, eight pretrained models and one attribution method (gradient magnitude); other methods were not tested.
  • Alignment needs a human map and the true label, so it is a diagnostic probe, not a deployable confidence score.
  • The model-level analysis rests on eight points, and the masked-pixel variant is post hoc.
  • ClickMe validation images are easier than full ImageNet, leaving few errors to analyse (103 for ViT-B/16).
